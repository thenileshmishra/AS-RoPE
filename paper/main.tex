\begin{abstract}
Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) \cite{su2021roformer} has become the de facto
positional encoding in modern neural machine translation and large language models,
yet its assumption that all frequency components contribute equally to position
representation has not been empirically validated. We introduce \textbf{Adaptive
RoPE}, a minimal extension that augments RoPE with learnable per-head frequency
gates and phase offsets. The gates are initialised to one (recovering standard
RoPE) and trained by gradient descent, allowing us to measure which frequency
components the model actually uses. Experiments on WMT14 English--German (3 seeds)
and Samanantar Hindi--English and Bengali--English (1 seed each) with an
encoder-decoder Transformer ($\approx$47M parameters) show three key findings:
(1)~trained gates consistently converge to values below one (mean~$\approx$0.90),
indicating a preference for low-frequency positional structure;
(2)~ablation studies reveal that frequency gates and phase offsets contribute
differently across language pairs, with phases particularly important for
Hindi--English;
(3)~while RoPE-based methods achieve the highest in-domain BLEU, ALiBi exhibits
superior length generalisation ($-$48\% degradation vs.~$-$62\% for RoPE on
out-of-distribution lengths). Adaptive RoPE matches standard RoPE in translation
quality at essentially zero computational overhead (+768 parameters per layer),
suggesting that RoPE's uniform frequency weighting is a suboptimal but
well-approximated inductive bias for machine translation.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
rotary position embedding, machine translation, positional encoding, frequency

\section{Introduction}

Position-aware attention is central to sequence-to-sequence models. Sinusoidal
absolute encodings \cite{vaswani2017attention} were the original solution, but
relative and rotary approaches have largely superseded them in modern
architectures \cite{su2021roformer,press2021train,raffel2020exploring}.

Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) \cite{su2021roformer} encodes position by
rotating query and key vectors in two-dimensional frequency planes. Each
dimension pair $(2i, 2i{+}1)$ is rotated by an angle $\theta_p^{(i)} = p \cdot
\omega_i$, where $\omega_i = 10000^{-2i/d}$ is a fixed frequency. Critically,
\emph{all} frequency components receive weight one --- no component is treated as
more or less important than another. RoPE is now the positional encoding of
choice in state-of-the-art language models \cite{touvron2023llama,jiang2023mistral}.

A recent study on decoder-only language models \cite{men2024roundandround}
showed empirically that high-frequency RoPE components contribute less to
attention than low-frequency ones and can be truncated without significant
quality loss. However, it is unknown whether the same frequency preference
appears in \emph{encoder-decoder} machine translation models, which differ
fundamentally in that positional information must serve both a bidirectional
encoder and a causal decoder with cross-attention. Moreover, no prior work has
disentangled the contribution of \emph{frequency gates} (scaling) from
\emph{phase offsets} (shifting) in RoPE adaptation.

We address this gap by designing Adaptive RoPE: a learnable probe that adds
trainable per-head gates $g_q, g_k \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times F}$ and phase
offsets $\phi_q, \phi_k \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times F}$ to the standard RoPE
rotation angles. When gates are fixed at one and phases at zero, Adaptive RoPE
is identical to RoPE. Trained by gradient descent, the gates reveal which
frequencies the model actually uses, while ablations (GatesOnly, PhasesOnly)
isolate the contribution of each component.

Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Adaptive RoPE}: a minimal, interpretable extension of RoPE with
        $4 \times H \times F$ learnable gates and phases (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{Frequency preference finding}: gates converge to ${\approx}0.90$
        in encoder-decoder MT, confirming low-frequency preference in a setting
        not studied by prior work (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{Cross-lingual ablations}: GatesOnly and PhasesOnly ablations
        reveal that phase offsets are more important for morphologically rich
        languages (Hi--En) while gates matter more for synthetic languages
        (Bn--En) (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{Length generalisation analysis}: ALiBi outperforms all RoPE
        variants on out-of-distribution lengths, while RoPE achieves the best
        in-domain BLEU (Section~\ref{sec:length}).
  \item \textbf{Per-head specialisation}: different attention heads learn
        distinct frequency profiles, revealing positional specialisation that
        standard RoPE prevents (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Positional encodings in MT.}
Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention} introduced sinusoidal absolute
encodings. Relative encodings \cite{shaw2018self,raffel2020exploring} replace
absolute positions with pairwise distances. ALiBi \cite{press2021train} adds a
fixed linear bias to attention logits based on token distance. RoPE
\cite{su2021roformer} achieves relative-position sensitivity through rotation of
Q/K vectors and has become the dominant choice due to its compatibility with
key-value caching and \texttt{torch.compile}.

\paragraph{Analysing RoPE.}
Men et al.~\cite{men2024roundandround} study which RoPE frequencies are
\emph{used} in decoder-only language models (Gemma~7B) and find that the highest
frequencies can be truncated with minimal perplexity loss. They propose p-RoPE,
a static pruning of high-frequency dimensions. Our work differs in four ways:
(i)~we study \emph{encoder-decoder} MT, not decoder-only LLMs; (ii)~we use a
\emph{differentiable, learnable} probe rather than a post-hoc ablation;
(iii)~we measure per-head frequency preferences across both encoder and decoder;
(iv)~we evaluate across \emph{multiple language pairs} (En--De, Hi--En, Bn--En).

\paragraph{Learnable RoPE variants.}
YaRN \cite{peng2023yarn} scales RoPE frequencies for length extrapolation.
LongRoPE \cite{ding2024longrope} learns per-dimension scaling factors for
extending context windows. These works target length generalisation; we target
understanding what frequencies are needed at training-time lengths, with
ablations that disentangle scaling from shifting.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)}

For a query vector $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ at position $p$, RoPE applies
a block-diagonal rotation:
\begin{equation}
  q'_{2i}   = q_{2i}\cos\theta_p^{(i)} - q_{2i+1}\sin\theta_p^{(i)},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  q'_{2i+1} = q_{2i}\sin\theta_p^{(i)} + q_{2i+1}\cos\theta_p^{(i)},
\end{equation}
where $\theta_p^{(i)} = p \cdot \omega_i$ and $\omega_i =
10000^{-2i/d}$. The same rotation is applied to keys $\mathbf{k}$, so the
inner product $\langle \mathbf{q}', \mathbf{k}' \rangle$ depends only on the
relative position $p - p'$ \cite{su2021roformer}.

\subsection{Adaptive RoPE}

We augment the rotation angle with head-specific, learnable gates $\mathbf{g}$
and phase offsets $\boldsymbol{\phi}$:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_p^{(h,i)} = p \cdot \omega_i \cdot g^{(h,i)} + \phi^{(h,i)},
  \label{eq:adaptive_rope}
\end{equation}
where $h \in \{1,\ldots,H\}$ indexes the attention head and $i \in
\{0,\ldots,F{-}1\}$ indexes the frequency ($F = d_{\text{head}}/2$). We
maintain separate parameters for queries and keys:
$\mathbf{g}_q, \mathbf{g}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times F}$ and
$\boldsymbol{\phi}_q, \boldsymbol{\phi}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times F}$.

\textbf{Initialisation.} $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{1}$, $\boldsymbol{\phi} =
\mathbf{0}$. This makes Adaptive RoPE \emph{identical} to standard RoPE at
initialisation, so any divergence reflects a genuine learned preference.

\textbf{Interpretation.} A gate $g^{(h,i)} < 1$ means head $h$ suppresses
frequency $i$ (the effective frequency is $\omega_i \cdot g^{(h,i)} < \omega_i$).
A gate $> 1$ amplifies it. Gates close to one indicate no preference.

\textbf{Parameter overhead.} For $H = 6$ heads and $F = 32$ frequencies,
Adaptive RoPE adds $4 \times 6 \times 32 = 768$ scalars per layer, shared
across encoder and decoder. Compared to 47M total parameters, this is
negligible ($<$0.002\%).

\subsection{Ablations}

To disentangle the contribution of gates and phases, we study two ablations:
\textbf{GatesOnly} has learnable gates ($\mathbf{g}$) with phases fixed at
zero; \textbf{PhasesOnly} has learnable phases ($\boldsymbol{\phi}$) with
gates fixed at one. Comparing these to full Adaptive RoPE and standard RoPE
reveals which component drives performance on each language pair.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets}

\textbf{WMT14 English--German} \cite{bojar2014wmt} is the standard
encoder-decoder MT benchmark, with 4.5M training pairs. Evaluation is on
\texttt{newstest2014} (3,003 sentences). Tokenisation uses the
\texttt{Helsinki-NLP/opus-mt-en-de} SentencePiece model \cite{tiedemann2020opus}
(vocabulary size 58,101).

\textbf{Samanantar Hindi--English} and \textbf{Bengali--English}
\cite{ramesh2022samanantar} are large-scale Indic MT datasets. We sample
1M training pairs for Hi--En and 450K for Bn--En. Test sets contain 3,000
sentences sampled from the official test splits. Tokenisation uses
\texttt{ai4bharat/IndicBART} \cite{dabre2021indicbart} (vocabulary size 64,000).

\subsection{Model Architecture}

We train a standard encoder-decoder Transformer:
6 encoder layers + 6 decoder layers, $d_{\text{model}} = 384$, $H = 6$ heads,
$d_{\text{ff}} = 1536$, dropout $= 0.1$, $\approx 47$M parameters.
Source and target embeddings are tied with the output projection.

\subsection{Training}

All models are trained for 25,000--30,000 steps with effective batch size 512
(batch 256, gradient accumulation 2), AdamW optimiser
\cite{loshchilov2018decoupled}, learning rate $10^{-3}$, cosine schedule with
5\% warmup, gradient clipping at 1.0. Mixed-precision (bf16) and
\texttt{torch.compile} \cite{pytorch2023} are enabled throughout.
Label smoothing $\varepsilon = 0.1$ is applied.

For En--De we report means across 3 random seeds (42, 43, 44); for Hi--En and
Bn--En we use seed 42 due to computational constraints.

\subsection{Evaluation}

Translation quality is measured with \textbf{BLEU} \cite{papineni2002bleu} and
\textbf{chrF} \cite{popovic2015chrf} via SacreBLEU \cite{post2018sacrebleu},
and \textbf{TER} for error rate. Decoding uses greedy decoding for the main
results; beam search ($b=5$) is used for comparison. Model calibration is
assessed via \textbf{validation loss} on a held-out subset.

Statistical significance is tested with paired $t$-tests on BLEU scores
across seeds (En--De) or bootstrap resampling (single-seed languages).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Translation Quality}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports BLEU scores for all positional encodings across
three language pairs. On En--De, RoPE achieves the highest mean BLEU
($20.83 \pm 0.03$), followed closely by PhasesOnly ($20.75 \pm 0.05$),
AdaptiveRoPE ($20.69 \pm 0.05$), and GatesOnly ($20.68 \pm 0.08$). The
differences among RoPE-based methods are small and not statistically
significant ($p > 0.05$), suggesting that the learned adaptations do not hurt
--- and in some cases marginally help --- translation quality.

Sinusoidal encoding ($20.22 \pm 0.15$) and ALiBi ($19.60$) lag behind RoPE
by $0.6$--$1.2$ BLEU. The RoPE vs.~ALiBi gap is statistically significant
($p = 0.021$), confirming that RoPE's rotary inductive bias is beneficial for
in-domain MT quality.

\subsection{Cross-Lingual Ablations}

The ablation results reveal striking language-dependent patterns:

\textbf{Hindi--English} (morphologically rich, free word order):
PhasesOnly achieves the highest BLEU ($9.42$), outperforming full AdaptiveRoPE
($9.08$) and GatesOnly ($8.37$). This suggests that phase offsets --- which
shift the positional rotation angle without changing frequency scale --- are
critical for capturing the flexible syntactic structure of Hindi. RoPE performs
worst ($7.78$), indicating that the fixed phase assumption hurts performance.

\textbf{Bengali--English} (synthetic morphology, relatively fixed word order):
AdaptiveRoPE achieves the highest BLEU ($18.01$), with GatesOnly ($17.79$)
close to RoPE ($17.87$). PhasesOnly drops to $17.06$, the lowest among
RoPE-based methods. This pattern is the \emph{opposite} of Hi--En: gates
(frequency scaling) matter more than phases for Bengali translation.

\textbf{English--German} (synthetic, rigid word order):
All RoPE variants cluster tightly ($20.68$--$20.83$), with PhasesOnly
marginally ahead of full AdaptiveRoPE. Neither component dominates,
suggesting that for this language pair, RoPE's defaults are already
near-optimal and the learned adaptations provide only marginal gains.

These results demonstrate that the \emph{optimal} positional encoding strategy
is language-dependent: phase flexibility benefits morphologically rich
languages, while frequency scaling helps synthetic ones.

\section{Length Generalisation}
\label{sec:length}

While RoPE-based methods achieve the best in-domain BLEU, their performance on
out-of-distribution sequence lengths is a known concern \cite{su2021roformer}.
We evaluate all methods on 8 length buckets (1--20, 21--40, \ldots, 201--400
source words) from the WMT14 En--De test set.

\subsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:length} reports BLEU degradation relative to the shortest
bucket (1--20 words). ALiBi shows the best extrapolation: only $-48.42\%$
degradation at 201--400 words, compared to $-62.42\%$ for RoPE. Sinusoidal
encoding also generalises better ($-57.52\%$) than RoPE variants.

Among RoPE-based methods, the differences are small: GatesOnly ($-59.07\%$),
PhasesOnly ($-60.07\%$), AdaptiveRoPE ($-60.96\%$), and RoPE ($-62.42\%$).
The learned adaptations provide at most $3.4\%$ relative improvement in
extrapolation over standard RoPE --- insufficient to close the gap with ALiBi.

  \caption{BLEU degradation on out-of-distribution source lengths (En--De,
greedy decoding). Values are \% change relative to the 1--20 word bucket.}
  \label{tab:length}
  \centering

    \toprule
    \textbf{Method} & \textbf{81--120 words} & \textbf{201--400 words} \\
    \midrule

\subsection{Position Interpolation}

We also test Position Interpolation (PI) \cite{chen2023extending}, which scales
RoPE's base frequency to extend the effective context window. On in-domain test
sets, PI at 1.5$\times$, 2.0$\times$, and 3.0$\times$ scaling yields
negligible BLEU differences ($<0.1$) from unscaled RoPE, as expected since the
test sequences are within the training-length distribution.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Frequency Spectrum Shift}

Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum} shows the effective frequency spectrum for standard
RoPE (uniform gates $= 1$) and Adaptive RoPE (trained gates $\approx 0.90$).
The learned model consistently downscales the effective rotation frequency
across all dimensions, with a larger suppression at higher-frequency indices.
This means the model prefers \emph{longer-range} positional signals over
short-range ones, consistent with the need to align source and target sentences
that may have different word orders in English--German translation.

\subsection{Per-Head Gate Specialisation}

Figure~\ref{fig:gates} visualises the gate values per attention head and per

is a constraint the model would prefer to relax. Head 2 shows the smallest
deviation from 1.0 (least suppression), while head 0 shows the largest
suppression, suggesting specialisation into long-range vs.\ short-range

progressively relies more on positional information as representations become
more contextualised.

\section{Discussion}

The consistent convergence of gates to values below one across all heads and
layers suggests that RoPE's default frequency schedule (base = 10,000) allocates
too much weight to high-frequency positional information for machine translation.
Low-frequency components encode coarse, long-range positional structure, which is
critical for cross-lingual reordering (e.g., verb-final German sentences).

Our cross-lingual ablations reveal a nuanced picture: the \emph{optimal}
positional encoding is not universal. Phase offsets are critical for
Hindi--English (free word order, morphologically rich), while frequency gates
matter more for Bengali--English (synthetic morphology). This suggests that
future work should explore \emph{language-adaptive} positional encodings that
condition on typological features.

The length generalisation results present a trade-off: RoPE achieves the best
in-domain BLEU but degrades most rapidly on long sequences. ALiBi, despite
lower absolute BLEU, generalises better to OOD lengths. For deployment
scenarios with variable input lengths, ALiBi or hybrid approaches may be
preferable. The learned adaptations in Adaptive RoPE provide only marginal
extrapolation improvements, suggesting that frequency gating alone is
insufficient to address RoPE's length generalisation limitations.

Our findings are consistent with Men et al.~\cite{men2024roundandround}, who
show high-frequency RoPE dimensions can be truncated in decoder-only LLMs.
However, our differentiable probe reveals the \emph{degree} and
\emph{head-level structure} of this preference, not just its existence, and does
so in an encoder-decoder MT setting where the dynamic is different due to
bidirectional encoding and cross-attention.

\textbf{Limitations.} Experiments are conducted at a single scale (47M
parameters). While En--De results use 3 seeds, Hi--En and Bn--En use only 1 seed
due to computational constraints. Whether the frequency preference generalises
to larger models and more language pairs remains an open question.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented Adaptive RoPE, a minimal learnable extension of Rotary Position
Embedding that reveals how encoder-decoder MT models use the RoPE frequency
spectrum. Our key findings are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Trained frequency gates consistently converge below one
        (mean~$\approx 0.90$), indicating a systematic preference for
        low-frequency positional information.
  \item Ablations show that gates and phases contribute differently across
        language pairs: phases are critical for Hindi--English, gates for
        Bengali--English.
  \item RoPE achieves the best in-domain BLEU, but ALiBi generalises better
        to out-of-distribution lengths ($-48$\% vs.~$-62$\% degradation).
  \item Adaptive RoPE matches standard RoPE in translation quality at
        negligible computational cost (+768 parameters per layer).
\end{enumerate}
These results suggest that RoPE's uniform frequency assumption is suboptimal
for MT and motivate future work on language-adaptive frequency scheduling and
hybrid positional encodings that combine RoPE's in-domain accuracy with
ALiBi's extrapolation robustness.
