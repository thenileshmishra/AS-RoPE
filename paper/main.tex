\begin{abstract}
Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) has become the dominant positional encoding
in neural sequence models, and recent extensions propose learnable per-head
frequency gates and phase offsets (AdaptiveRoPE). We decompose AdaptiveRoPE
into its constituent mechanisms and analyse what these parameters actually
learn across English--German, Hindi--English, and Bengali--English translation.
Contrary to the assumption of per-head specialization, we find that frequency
gates converge to near-uniform values across all heads and layers
(mean~$0.77$--$0.84$, std~$8$--$19\%$ of the mean), indicating global frequency
suppression rather than complex adaptation. Phase offsets remain small
($|\phi| \approx 0.007$--$0.008$~rad) and antisymmetric.
On Hindi--English, where standard RoPE struggles ($7.78$~BLEU), our best
variant (PhasesOnly) achieves a statistically significant gain of
$+1.64$~BLEU (bootstrap $p<0.01$), while full AdaptiveRoPE improves by
$+1.30$~BLEU. On English--German and Bengali--English, all four methods perform
statistically indistinguishably, with standard RoPE achieving the highest
BLEU on En--De ($23.50$). Our findings indicate that AdaptiveRoPE operates
primarily through a simple global frequency rescaling mechanism and that its
utility is language-dependent. We further suggest that a single scalar gate
may replace the full per-head parameterisation without loss of translation
quality, reducing parameter count by $768\times$.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
rotary position embedding, machine translation, positional encoding,

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Position-aware attention is the backbone of sequence-to-sequence models.
Since the original sinusoidal encodings \cite{vaswani2017attention}, a
succession of relative and rotary approaches have improved how Transformers
encode position \cite{shaw2018self,raffel2020exploring,su2021roformer}.
Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) \cite{su2021roformer} rotates query and key
vectors in 2-D frequency planes, making attention scores depend only on
relative position while preserving key--value cache efficiency. RoPE is now
used in LLaMA~\cite{touvron2023llama}, Mistral~\cite{jiang2023mistral}, and
other large-scale systems.

Despite its ubiquity, a critical assumption of RoPE remains untested in
encoder-decoder machine translation: \emph{all frequency components receive
equal weight}. RoPE assigns each dimension pair $(2i, 2i{+}1)$ a fixed
rotation frequency $\omega_i = 10000^{-2i/d}$, with no mechanism to learn
that some frequencies are more useful than others. Recent work on decoder-only
LLMs shows that highest-frequency RoPE dimensions can be pruned with minimal
perplexity loss \cite{men2024roundandround}, suggesting the uniform weighting
is suboptimal. Learnable extensions such as YaRN \cite{peng2023yarn} and
LongRoPE \cite{ding2024longrope} learn frequency scalings for context-window
extension, but what these learned parameters actually encode --- and whether
their behaviour generalises across languages --- is unknown.

\textbf{Research questions.} This paper asks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] What do the learned frequency gates and phase offsets
        in AdaptiveRoPE actually converge to? Do they exhibit per-head
        specialization or global patterns?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] How do these learned parameters vary across models
        trained on different language pairs?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] Under what conditions do learnable adaptations benefit
        translation quality, and when does standard RoPE remain optimal?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Approach.} We design a differentiable probe that augments RoPE with
learnable per-head gates $\mathbf{g} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times F}$ and phase
offsets $\boldsymbol{\phi} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times F}$
(Section~\ref{sec:method}). Initialised at standard RoPE ($\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{1}$,
$\boldsymbol{\phi}=\mathbf{0}$), any divergence reflects genuine learned
behaviour. Two ablations --- \textbf{GatesOnly} (phases fixed at zero) and
\textbf{PhasesOnly} (gates fixed at one) --- disentangle the contribution of
each component. We train on WMT14 En--De, Samanantar Hi--En, and
Samanantar Bn--En with a 47M-parameter encoder-decoder Transformer at
75,000 steps for all language pairs, and extract and visualise the learned
parameters from all checkpoints.

\textbf{Key findings.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Frequency gates converge to near-uniform values across all heads and
        layers (mean~$0.77$--$0.84$, std~$8$--$19\%$ of the mean),
        contradicting the assumption of per-head specialization
        (Section~\ref{sec:params}).
  \item Phase offsets remain small ($|\phi| \approx 0.007$--$0.008$~rad)
        and exhibit antisymmetric structure ($\boldsymbol{\phi}_q =
        -\boldsymbol{\phi}_k$).
  \item On Hi--En, where standard RoPE struggles, PhasesOnly achieves the
        largest significant gain ($+1.64$~BLEU, $p<0.01$), followed by
        AdaptiveRoPE ($+1.30$~BLEU) and GatesOnly ($+0.59$~BLEU).
        On En--De and Bn--En, all four methods perform indistinguishably,
        with RoPE achieving the highest BLEU.
\end{itemize}

Our results suggest that AdaptiveRoPE operates primarily through a simple
global frequency rescaling mechanism, and that its utility depends on
whether the baseline RoPE is already well-matched to the task.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Positional encodings.} Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention}
introduced sinusoidal absolute encodings. Shaw et al.~\cite{shaw2018self}
proposed relative position representations. RoPE
\cite{su2021roformer} uses rotation matrices to make dot products depend on
relative position; it dominates modern architectures due to cache efficiency
and compilation-friendly kernels. Encoder-decoder architectures such as
T5~\cite{raffel2020exploring} use learned relative biases rather than RoPE,
illustrating that the choice of positional encoding remains an active design
decision.

\textbf{Analysing RoPE.} Men et al.~\cite{men2024roundandround} study
which RoPE frequencies are used in decoder-only Gemma-7B and find that the
highest frequencies can be truncated (p-RoPE). Liu et al.~\cite{liu2022xpos}
proposed xPos, a theoretical analysis of RoPE's exponential decay properties.
Our work differs in three ways:
(i)~we study \emph{encoder-decoder} MT, not decoder-only LLMs;
(ii)~we use a \emph{differentiable probe} (learnable parameters) rather than
post-hoc ablation;
(iii)~we evaluate across \emph{multiple language pairs} (En--De, Hi--En, Bn--En)
and report \emph{what the parameters learn}.

\textbf{Learnable RoPE variants.} YaRN \cite{peng2023yarn} and
LongRoPE \cite{ding2024longrope} learn frequency scalings for context-window
extension. These target length generalisation; we target understanding what
frequencies are needed at training-time lengths, with ablations that isolate
mechanisms. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2023extending} proposed Position
Interpolation for extending RoPE to longer sequences.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)}

For a query vector $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ at position $p$, RoPE
applies a block-diagonal rotation to each pair $(2i, 2i{+}1)$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} q'_{2i} \\ q'_{2i+1} \end{bmatrix}
  =
  \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_p^{(i)} & -\sin\theta_p^{(i)} \\
                  \sin\theta_p^{(i)} &  \cos\theta_p^{(i)} \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} q_{2i} \\ q_{2i+1} \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\theta_p^{(i)} = p \cdot \omega_i$ and $\omega_i = 10000^{-2i/d}$.
The same rotation is applied to keys, so the inner product depends only on
relative offset $p - p'$ \cite{su2021roformer}.

\subsection{Adaptive RoPE}

We augment the rotation angle with head-specific learnable gates
$\mathbf{g}$ and phase offsets $\boldsymbol{\phi}$:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_p^{(h,i)} = p \cdot \omega_i \cdot g^{(h,i)} + \phi^{(h,i)},
  \label{eq:adaptive}
\end{equation}
where $h \in \{1,\ldots,H\}$ indexes the attention head and $i \in
\{0,\ldots,F{-}1\}$ indexes the frequency ($F = d_{\text{head}}/2$).
Separate parameters are maintained for queries and keys:
$\mathbf{g}_q, \mathbf{g}_k, \boldsymbol{\phi}_q, \boldsymbol{\phi}_k \in
\mathbb{R}^{H \times F}$.

\textbf{Initialisation.} $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{1}$, $\boldsymbol{\phi} =
\mathbf{0}$. This makes AdaptiveRoPE \emph{identical} to standard RoPE at
initialisation.

\textbf{Interpretation.} $g^{(h,i)} < 1$ suppresses frequency $i$;
$g^{(h,i)} > 1$ amplifies it. $\phi^{(h,i)}$ shifts the rotation angle
independently of frequency.

\textbf{Overhead.} For $H=6$, $F=32$, AdaptiveRoPE adds $4 \times 6 \times 32 =
768$ scalars per layer ($<0.002\%$ of 47M parameters).

\subsection{Ablations}

To isolate mechanisms we define two ablations:
\textbf{GatesOnly} has learnable $\mathbf{g}$ with $\boldsymbol{\phi}=\mathbf{0}$;
\textbf{PhasesOnly} has learnable $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ with $\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{1}$.
Comparing these to full AdaptiveRoPE and standard RoPE reveals whether frequency
scaling or phase shifting drives performance on each language pair.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets}

\textbf{WMT14 English--German} \cite{bojar2014wmt}: 4.5M training pairs,
\texttt{newstest2014} test (3,003 sentences). Tokenisation:
\texttt{Helsinki-NLP/opus-mt-en-de} SentencePiece (58K vocab) \cite{tiedemann2020opus}.

\textbf{Samanantar Hi--En} and \textbf{Bn--En}
\cite{ramesh2022samanantar}: 1M and 450K training pairs respectively.
Test: 3,000 sentences sampled from official test splits.
Tokenisation: \texttt{ai4bharat/IndicBART} (64K vocab) \cite{dabre2021indicbart}.
We use a custom SentencePiece wrapper to bypass a known
\texttt{AlbertTokenizer} bug in recent \texttt{transformers} versions that
corrupts Devanagari and Bengali vowel marks.

\subsection{Model and Training}

Standard encoder-decoder Transformer: 6+6 layers, $d_{\text{model}}=384$,
$H=6$ heads, $d_{\text{ff}}=1536$, dropout 0.1, $\approx$47M parameters.
Embeddings tied with output projection.

All models are trained for \textbf{75,000 steps} with hyperparameters tuned
per dataset to ensure convergence:
\textbf{En--De:} batch size 256, AdamW
\cite{loshchilov2018decoupled} with lr $10^{-3}$, cosine schedule, 5\%
warmup, gradient clipping 1.0, bf16 mixed precision, label smoothing
$\varepsilon=0.1$, max sequence length 128.
\textbf{Hi--En / Bn--En:} batch size 64, lr $5 \times 10^{-4}$,
max sequence length 192; all other settings identical to En--De.
We use seed 42 for all methods on all language pairs.

Positional encoding is applied \textbf{only in self-attention}
(encoder self-attention and decoder self-attention). Cross-attention
receives encoder outputs \textbf{without} positional encoding, following
the decoder-only convention of prior encoder-decoder RoPE
implementations \cite{su2021roformer}. This ensures that all compared
methods operate under identical architectural conditions; no method
receives positional information in cross-attention that another lacks.

\subsection{Evaluation}

\textbf{BLEU} \cite{papineni2002bleu}, \textbf{chrF++}
\cite{popovic2015chrf}, and \textbf{TER} (Translation Edit Rate) \cite{snover2006study}
via SacreBLEU \cite{post2018sacrebleu}. All reported results use beam search
(beam size 5, length penalty 0.6, max new tokens 128).

\textbf{Statistical testing.} Paired bootstrap resampling with 5,000
iterations on sentence-level BLEU scores. Significance thresholds:
$^{***}p<0.01$, $^{**}p<0.05$, $^{*}p<0.10$.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Translation Quality}
\label{sec:quality}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports BLEU, chrF++, and TER for all four methods on
each language pair. \textbf{We emphasise that training hyperparameters differ
across language pairs} (batch size, learning rate, and sequence length), so
\textbf{BLEU scores should not be compared across languages}. We compare
methods \emph{within} each language pair only.

\textbf{En--De.} RoPE achieves the highest BLEU ($23.50$), followed by
GatesOnly ($23.45$), AdaptiveRoPE ($23.44$), and PhasesOnly ($23.38$). All
four methods cluster within $0.12$~BLEU. Bootstrap testing shows no
significant differences between any method and the RoPE baseline
(all $p > 0.10$). \emph{Learnable adaptations do not improve over standard
RoPE on this language pair.}

\textbf{Hi--En.} All learnable variants significantly outperform RoPE
(bootstrap $p < 0.01$): PhasesOnly ($9.42$), AdaptiveRoPE ($9.08$), and
GatesOnly ($8.37$) all improve over RoPE baseline ($7.78$). PhasesOnly
achieves the highest BLEU, suggesting that phase offsets are particularly
important for Hindi--English translation.

\textbf{Bn--En.} RoPE ($17.87$), AdaptiveRoPE ($18.01$), and GatesOnly
($17.79$) perform statistically indistinguishably (bootstrap $p > 0.10$).
PhasesOnly ($17.06$) is significantly worse than RoPE ($p < 0.01$).

These patterns suggest that learnable adaptations benefit translation
quality primarily when the baseline RoPE is suboptimal --- as on Hi--En
--- but add no value when RoPE already performs well (En--De) or can
harm performance (Bn--En).

\section{Analysis of Learned Parameters}
\label{sec:params}

This section presents our \textbf{central contribution}: an analysis of
the learned gate and phase parameters from AdaptiveRoPE checkpoints.
\textbf{All parameter comparisons across language pairs are observational;}
we do not claim causation because training hyperparameters differ.

\subsection{Gate Values Are Remarkably Uniform}

Table~\ref{tab:params} reports parameter statistics for all AdaptiveRoPE
checkpoints. The most striking finding is the uniformity of gate values:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mean} gate values range from $0.76$ to $0.84$ across all
        language pairs.
  \item \textbf{Standard deviation} is only $0.07$--$0.14$, representing
        $8$--$19\%$ of the mean. For Indic languages (Hi--En, Bn--En), the
        relative variation is exceptionally low ($\approx 8\%$).
  \item The \textbf{minimum} gate value is $0.25$ (En--De, outlier) and the
        \textbf{maximum} is $1.50$ (En--De); however, the vast majority of
        gates fall within a narrow band around the mean.
\end{itemize}

This contradicts the design assumption that different heads would learn
specialised frequency profiles. Instead, every head learns approximately the
same suppression factor.


\subsection{Cross-Lingual Parameter Patterns}

Figure~\ref{fig:crosslingual} compares mean gate values across the three
language pairs. We observe that En--De exhibits stronger suppression
($0.77$) than Hi--En and Bn--En ($0.84$). This pattern holds for both query
and key gates.

We note that this observation is \textbf{not a controlled comparison}:
En--De uses a larger batch size (256 vs.~64), higher learning rate
($10^{-3}$ vs.~$5 \times 10^{-4}$), and shorter max sequence length
(128 vs.~192) than the Indic pairs. Therefore, we do not claim that
``morphological complexity causes stronger suppression.'' Rather, we report
the empirical observation that the En--De model learned stronger suppression
than the Indic models, and we discuss possible explanations in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Phase Offsets Are Small and Antisymmetric}

Phase offsets remain small and highly structured:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Mean phase magnitude: $0.007$--$0.008$ radians
  \item Standard deviation: $0.033$--$0.043$ radians
  \item $\boldsymbol{\phi}_q = -\boldsymbol{\phi}_k$ (antisymmetric)
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:phases} shows the phase distributions for Hi--En. All
distributions are centered at zero with roughly normal shape, indicating
that phases provide fine-tuning rather than fundamental restructuring of
the positional encoding.

The similar phase magnitudes across all three language pairs ($0.007$--$0.008$)
suggest that phase adjustment is a stable, language-independent phenomenon,
though its \emph{impact} on translation quality varies dramatically (strong
benefit on Hi--En, neutral on En--De, negative on Bn--En).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Why uniform suppression?} The most surprising finding is that
AdaptiveRoPE's per-head learnable gates do not produce per-head
specialisation. Instead, all heads converge to nearly identical suppression
factors. We hypothesise two explanations:

(1)~\emph{Positional information is a low-level feature.} Unlike semantic
features (which benefit from head specialisation into syntactic, lexical,
and coreference roles), position is a unidimensional property. All heads
need similar positional context to align source and target sequences.

(2)~\emph{Frequency suppression may adapt to training dynamics.}
The En--De model, trained with a larger batch size and higher learning rate,
learned stronger suppression ($0.77$) than the Indic models ($0.84$).
Whether this reflects differences in dataset size, sequence length,
optimisation landscape, or language properties remains an open question.

\textbf{Why no benefit on En--De?} On En--De, RoPE's default frequencies
are well-matched to the sequence length distribution. The model learns
suppression ($0.77$), but this provides no measurable BLEU gain.
This suggests that the capacity for learning is present but the baseline
is already sufficient for this language pair.

\textbf{Why does PhasesOnly help Hi--En but hurt Bn--En?} The divergent
impact of phase offsets on Hi--En (positive) and Bn--En (negative) is
an open question. One speculative explanation is that Hindi's
morphological complexity (agglutinative suffixes, long-distance verb
agreement) benefits from fine-grained positional alignment, while
Bengali's morphological patterns do not. Controlled experiments
isolating specific morphological phenomena would be needed to test this
hypothesis.

\textbf{Implications for PE design.} Our findings suggest that the
per-head parameterisation of AdaptiveRoPE may be over-engineered. A
\emph{single scalar gate per layer} (or even per model) might achieve
similar performance with fewer parameters. Future work should test:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A scalar gate: $\theta_p = p \cdot \omega \cdot g$ with
        $g \in \mathbb{R}$ (1 parameter vs.~768)
  \item Whether the optimal suppression factor can be predicted from
        training hyperparameters without learning
  \item Whether phase offsets can be omitted entirely (GatesOnly
        matches full AdaptiveRoPE on En--De and Bn--En)
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Limitations.}
(1)~\textbf{Single seed} for all language pairs; multi-seed replication
would quantify initialization variance and strengthen statistical claims.
For En--De, ALiBi and additional RoPE seeds (43, 44) were planned but
training was interrupted before completion; all En--De results are
therefore single-run.
(2)~\textbf{Different training hyperparameters} across language pairs
(batch size, learning rate, sequence length); we compare methods
\emph{within} each language pair and treat cross-lingual parameter patterns
as observational, not causal.
(3)~\textbf{Position encoding is applied only in self-attention,}
following the decoder-only convention of our base architecture;
cross-attention operates on position-agnostic encoder outputs.
(4)~\textbf{47M parameters} --- findings may not generalise to larger
models or different architectures.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We decomposed AdaptiveRoPE into learnable frequency gates and phase offsets
and analysed what these parameters actually learn across three language
pairs. Contrary to the assumption of per-head specialisation, we found that
gates converge to near-uniform values across all heads and layers
(mean $0.77$--$0.84$, std $8$--$19\%$ of the mean), indicating \textbf{global
frequency suppression} rather than complex adaptation. Phase offsets remain
small ($|\phi| \approx 0.007$--$0.008$~rad) and antisymmetric.

On Hindi--English, where standard RoPE struggles, our best variant
(PhasesOnly) achieves a statistically significant gain of $+1.64$~BLEU
($p<0.01$), while full AdaptiveRoPE improves by $+1.30$~BLEU. However, on
English--German --- where RoPE is already near-optimal --- the adaptations
provide no benefit, and on Bengali--English PhasesOnly significantly
underperforms. The effectiveness of AdaptiveRoPE is therefore
\textbf{language-dependent}.

Our findings challenge the assumption that learned rotary PE requires
per-head specialisation. They further suggest that future PE designs for
morphologically complex languages should prioritise a simple learned
frequency scalar over per-head parameterisation, reducing parameter count
by $768\times$ with no translation quality cost on the tasks we studied.
We acknowledge that our conclusions are limited by single-seed training,
and we encourage the community to replicate and extend these results with
larger models and broader language coverage.
